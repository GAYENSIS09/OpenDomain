                                                  Abstract
         ——————————————————————————– 
     We present OpenDomain, a domain-first, evidence-driven software engineering framework for use with AI coding 
     assistants (“vibe coding”). The framework addresses a specific and increasingly common failure mode:
     an AI assistant can produce syntactically complete, internally consistent, test-passing software
     whose domain semantics are invented rather than known. The failure is not a modelling error; it
     is an epistemic error. The system has no representation of what is known, what is assumed, what
     is inferred, and what is unknown, and therefore no way to distinguish a reconstructed domain
     rule from a plausible hallucination. OpenDomain contributes (C1) a closed epistemic lattice
     attached to every engineering artifact, permitting EXPLICIT, TACIT, HYBRID, INFERRED,
     ASSUMED, UNKNOWN and CONFLICTING knowledge to be represented, preserved and
     enforced as distinct states rather than collapsed into a single asserted specification; (C2) an
     evidence-gated derivation protocol that structurally forbids promoting an external statement
     directly into a requirement, requiring the intermediate steps Source → Finding → Interpretation
     → Candidate Need → Requirement; (C3) a single ordered traceability spine spanning source,
     evidence, domain knowledge, requirement, use case, specification, acceptance criterion, contract,
     plan, task, code, test and validation result, over which two query classes – “Why does this exist?”
     and “If this changes, what breaks?” – become mechanically answerable; (C4) nine deterministic,
     side-effect-free quality gates that detect epistemic laundering, evidence-free requirements and
     dangling traceability links before implementation begins; (C5) a first-class opportunity stage
     that formalises innovation as a falsifiable, kill-criteria-governed engineering process, preventing
     feature replication from masquerading as analysis; and (C6) an explicit formal rejection of the
     Minimum Viable Product as a scope-governing device, replaced by evidence-bounded capability
     classification and evidence-complete vertical increments. Two contributions concern verification
     specifically. (C7) a three-loop process that nests specification-driven, behavioural-driven and
     test-driven development as concentric loops with different units of verification, different artifacts
     and different definitions of done, together with a rigour-by-requirement-type table that makes
     the choice of rigour an explicit, reviewable declaration rather than an unstated habit. (C8) a
     provisioning subsystem that detects the project’s language stack, selects a verification toolchain
     by a deterministic rule, installs it locally and idempotently at pinned versions, generates the
     executable specifications and step definitions, binds each verification artifact to the acceptance
     criterion it checks with recorded binding provenance, and refuses to proceed when the declared
     rigour cannot be honoured – while recording, for every specification, whether it was actually
     executed, merely discovered, or only declared. We formalise the framework as a nine-gate
     pipeline with explicit transition preconditions, specify a reference implementation architecture
     in which all gate evaluation is a pure function of a version-controlled workspace, and define an
     evaluation protocol comprising four studies: a longitudinal case study, a controlled comparison
     against specification-first development, a falsification-oriented survey of constructed domain
     artefacts, and a mechanised audit of epistemic-state transitions. We report the framework

 1 INTRODUCTION
 1.1 The setting
 AI coding assistants have changed the entry cost of software production. A practitioner with limited
 or no software engineering training can now produce a running application from a natural-language
 description. This is a genuine democratisation of construction.
    It has not democratised understanding.
    The distinction matters because software engineering is not principally the activity of producing
 code. It is the activity of producing a defensible answer to the question “what should exist, and
 why?”. A codebase that does not correspond to any defensible answer to that question is not

                                                   6
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                                             1   INTRODUCTION



software; it is an artefact that behaves like software. The cost of such an artefact is deferred, not
avoided: it surfaces during integration with the real world, during regulatory review, during staff
turnover, and during the first requirement change that nobody anticipated.

1.2 The specific failure mode
We name the failure mode this paper addresses: semantic fabrication under plausible presentation.
   An AI assistant asked to “add invoice management” will produce entities (Invoice, LineItem,
Customer), a state machine, a REST API, migrations, and tests. These artifacts are internally
coherent. They will also be, in the absence of explicit constraint:
• a reimplementation of the most common public invoice schema, not the schema of the deploying
  organisation;
• a lifecycle with four states, because four is the modal answer, not because the deploying
  organisation’s fiscal and legal constraints imply four;
• tax handling that is plausible and non-compliant in the target jurisdiction;
• an approval workflow copied from competitor documentation, not derived from the actors’
  actual decision rights.
    None of this is hallucination in the strict sense. Each individual statement is statistically
reasonable. The defect is that the assistant has no representational slot in which to record that it
does not know. It cannot distinguish:

"In Senegal, a facture normalisee must carry a NINEA and a registration
 number, per the Code general des impots" (known, and checkable)


   from

"Invoices probably need approval states" (inferred from training
 distribution, and not checkable).


   Both are emitted in the same register. The consumer of the output has no way to tell them
apart. This is the core problem.

1.3 Why existing process knowledge does not solve it
The domain engineering, requirements engineering and specification-driven development literatures
already contain most of the conceptual apparatus needed to solve this. What they do not contain
is a mechanism that is simultaneously:

(a) machine-enforceable, so that an autonomous agent cannot bypass it by
    omission;
(b) cheap enough to be applied per-change rather than per-project;
(c) explicit about ignorance, so that uncertainty is a representable and
    reportable first-class state;
(d) agnostic to whether the specification is human-authored or
    agent-authored.


    Process documents fail (a). Static analysis fails (c). Prompt engineering fails (b) and (d).
Traceability matrices fail (b) unless automated. We argue that the missing element is not a new
process stage but a new representational substrate: a machine-checkable artifact model in which
epistemic state is part of the data rather than a matter of author discipline.

1.4 What this paper contributes
Contribution summary, stated conservatively. Each item is a design contribution; none is an
empirical result.
C1. The epistemic lattice. A closed, seven-valued classification attached to
    every artifact, with no "default" value, such that an artifact cannot
    exist without declaring how it is known.

C2. The evidence gate on derivation. A structural constraint forbidding the
    promotion of an external statement to a requirement without the
    intermediate record of finding, interpretation and candidate need.

C3. The traceability spine. A single ordered artifact chain with a fixed
    relation vocabulary, supporting bidirectional query.

C4. Nine deterministic quality gates. Pure functions over a workspace, with
    no side effects, making gate results reproducible and auditable.

C5. The opportunity stage as a mandatory, hypothesis-governed stage,
    separating "what is true of the world" from "what we believe could be
    better", and making the latter falsifiable.

C6. The formal rejection of MVP-as-scope-device, and its replacement.

C7. Rigour selection as a function of requirement type rather than a global
    methodological mandate.

1.5 Scope
OpenDomain is a method and a reference implementation of that method. It is not a code
generator, not a requirements management suite, and not a replacement for domain expert
judgement. Where expert judgement is available, OpenDomain records and structures it; it does
not manufacture it. Where it is unavailable, OpenDomain reconstructs from evidence and marks
the residue as UNKNOWN. The framework’s central claim is not that it eliminates ignorance but
that it makes ignorance legible.

2 PROBLEM STATEMENT
2.1 Formal statement
Let D be a real-world domain. Let K be the knowledge an engineering process holds about D.
Standard practice produces software S as a function of K without requiring K to be complete,
explicit, or internally consistent:
S = f(K)      where K may be partial, tacit, contradictory, or absent

   Under this regime the following are undecidable at any point in the lifecycle:
(Q1) Provenance.  For any element s in S, what observation or authority
                 justifies s?
(Q2) Necessity. For any element s in S, is s required by the domain, or
                 has it been introduced by convention, analogy to a
                 competitor, or by an agent's prior?
(Q3) Blast radius. For any change c to a requirement r, which downstream
                 artifacts become invalid?
(Q4) Trust.      For any element s in S, is s confirmed, inferred, assumed,
                 or unknown?

    We claim that Q1-Q4 are undecidable not because the information is unavailable but because
the process has no representation in which to hold it. Information that is not represented is not
recoverable.

2.2 The under-specified agent
The agent setting sharpens the problem. A human engineer applying domain engineering methods
has, at minimum, tacit access to the organisation around them: they overhear a conversation,
they see a form nobody uses, they know which manager does not read email. An AI assistant has
no such access. Its entire epistemic endowment is its training distribution plus whatever the user
supplies.
    This produces a characteristic and dangerous asymmetry: the agent is maximally fluent
precisely where it is maximally ignorant. Fluency is generated by the model; ignorance is not
visible in the output because the output is fluent. Where a junior human engineer’s ignorance is
diagnosable – they hesitate, they ask, they get the terminology wrong – the agent’s ignorance is
syntactically indistinguishable from its competence.
    Any framework intended for agentic use must therefore make ignorance representable and
checkable, not merely discouraged. This is the design constraint from which most of OpenDomain
follows.

2.3 Two concrete failure instances
Failure instance 1 – the tax rate. An assistant implements sales tax. The target jurisdiction uses
value-added tax with a different rate structure, a different invoicing obligation, and possibly a
different fiscal year boundary. The implementation is clean, tested, and wrong. The requirement
“the system SHALL compute tax” has no jurisdiction, and the system has no notion that tax is
jurisdiction-scoped. Nothing in the codebase records that this is unresolved.
   Failure instance 2 – the approval step. An assistant adds status: DRAFT → PEND-
ING_APPROVAL → APPROVED, copied from competitor documentation. In the target
organisation, approval authority belongs to a role that the assistant has never heard of, and the
approval is legally required to be performed by a named individual with a signature obligation.
The state machine is a plausible fiction. The requirement derived from it will be “traceable” to a
source that is, on inspection, a competitor’s marketing page.
   In both cases the conventional remedy – “write better requirements” – is ineffective, because
the requirement was never wrong in the sense of being ambiguous. It was wrong in the sense of
being unfounded, and nothing in its form revealed that.

3 RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND DESIGN OBJECTIVES
3.1 The two motivating goals
The framework is designed against two goals, stated by the originating practitioner and retained
unchanged because they are the purpose rather than an intermediate means.

G1 (Comprehension support). To enable a practitioner using an AI coding
   assistant -- including one without formal training in the domain -- to
   build an accurate, evidence-backed understanding of an unfamiliar domain,
   using systematic external research rather than the assistant's priors.


G2 (Problem discovery). To enable that practitioner, having reached the
   comprehension of a domain competent practitioner, to identify problems
   that the domain's own actors have not articulated -- because they are
   structural, latent, or outside the frame within which the actors think --
   and to formulate technology-mediated responses to them.


   These goals are sequential and are not collapsed. G1 produces a validated domain model. G2
operates on that model, and its output is a set of falsifiable hypotheses, not a feature list. G3


                                                 9
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering           3   RESEARCH QUESTIONS AND DESIGN OBJECTIVES



(not stated by the practitioner but implied by the deliverable) is to produce a specification and
implementation whose every element is traceable to G1 or G2.
   The relationship is:

G1: What is true of this domain?
G2: Given what is true, what should be questioned?
G3: Given what should be questioned, what exactly will be built, and can we
    demonstrate that what was built does that?


3.2 Research questions
RQ1. Can a machine-checkable artifact model, in which epistemic state is part
     of the data, make it possible for an autonomous agent to preserve
     uncertainty through an entire engineering lifecycle rather than
     collapsing it into confident prose?


RQ2. Does requiring a structured derivation chain between external evidence
     and requirement reduce the rate of unfounded requirements in agent-
     authored specifications?


RQ3. What is the minimum set of mechanically checkable gate conditions that
     detects epistemic laundering (the progressive promotion of ASSUMED or
     INFERRED knowledge to VALIDATED) and evidence-free requirements?


RQ4. Can the addition of a mandatory, hypothesis-governed opportunity stage
     reduce feature replication -- the reproduction of competitor capability
     presented as analysis -- in agent-generated specifications?


RQ5. Does a formal rejection of MVP-as-scope-device, replaced by
     evidence-bounded capability classification, improve the long-run
     coherence of agent-generated systems relative to MVP-scoped systems?


RQ6. Is the framework's overhead tolerable? That is: is the marginal cost of
     evidence and traceability maintenance lower than the expected cost of
     semantic fabrication, at the granularity of a single change?


  RQ1-RQ3 are primarily design questions answered by construction and evaluation protocol.
RQ4-RQ6 are empirical and are stated here as open.

3.3 Non-goals
• OpenDomain does not assert that AI coding assistants are net harmful. It asserts that they
  amplify the cost of an already-existing epistemic gap.
• OpenDomain does not replace expert judgement. Where an expert is available, the framework’s
  cost is close to zero; the marginal value is in recording and enforcing the expert’s own traceability.
• OpenDomain does not adopt microservices, event sourcing, CQRS, or any other architectural
  style. Architecture is derived from domain boundaries and quality attributes, never from
  fashion (Section 5.9).
• OpenDomain does not aim to maximise the number of artifacts. It enforces a minimal-artifact
  principle (Section 5.11): an artifact exists only if it provides clarity, traceability, verification,
  coordination, a decision record, or maintenance value.

4 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
4.1 Domain knowledge in requirements engineering
Araujo et al. [1] report a systematic mapping study of domain knowledge in requirements
engineering, screening 75 papers against inclusion criteria. Three findings of that study are
load-bearing for this work.
    First, domain knowledge is established as a key component of RE: it supplies the conceptual
support needed to understand system context, align with stakeholder needs, and reduce ambiguity
in specification. This paper does not contest that finding; it treats it as the premise.
    Second, the study identifies recurring challenges in the formalisation, acquisition, and long-
term maintenance of domain knowledge. This is precisely where the agentic setting bites hardest.
Acquisition, in the practitioner’s absence of experts, becomes a research activity; formalisation
becomes a language-design activity; and maintenance becomes an impact-analysis activity. All
three are activities that a human performs slowly and an agent performs fast and badly.
    Third, the study’s stated future work emphasises “scalable, automated, and sustainable
solutions to integrate domain knowledge into RE processes.” The scalability qualifier is the crux.
Most existing domain-knowledge approaches are project-scale activities performed by a team over
weeks. An agent operates at change-scale, continuously, without a team. A method optimised for
the former regime cannot be simply transferred to the latter.
   Our position: the sustainability problem named by [1] is an engineering problem with an
engineering answer. It is solved by making knowledge artifacts version-controlled, incrementally
maintained, and machine-checked – not by adding ceremony.

4.2 Specification-driven development
Piskala [2] provides a practitioner’s guide to specification-driven development (SDD), inverting
the traditional workflow so that specifications rather than code are the primary artifact. Three
elements of that work are adopted directly.
    The first is the tiered notion of specification authority: spec-first (specification then code),
spec-anchored (specification and code co-evolve under mutual constraint, the default for production
systems), and spec-as-source (specification generates code, applicable only where generation tooling
is trustworthy). We adopt this tiering because it correctly identifies that specification strictness is
a function of consequence and duration, not of methodological taste. We note explicitly the paper’s
own warning that LLM-generated application code should not be treated as spec-as-source: an
agent’s generation is a proposal requiring verification, not a derivation with guaranteed correctness.
    The second is the separation of stages – specification, plan, task, implementation – which
the paper treats as mandatory. This paper’s contribution is to extend that chain upstream of
specification, to the evidence that justifies the requirements the specification consumes, and
laterally, with an opportunity stage that the SDD literature does not include.
    The third is the paper’s identification of false confidence as a major pitfall: software can
faithfully implement an incorrect specification, so passing tests do not establish correctness. This
paper accepts the observation and asks the question the paper leaves open: how is the correctness
of the specification itself established, given that the specification is frequently agent-authored?
Our answer is that it cannot be established by the agent, and that the correct system behaviour
is to record the absence of establishment rather than to imply it.

4.3 Domain-specific modelling languages and MBSE
Beers et al. [3] address a closely adjacent problem for production systems. Their work introduces
a domain-specific modelling language (DSML) for the conceptual design phase, built on the SPES

OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                         4   BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK



(Software Platform Embedded Systems) framework and extending SysML through the UML profile
mechanism, with viewpoint-specific diagram types and automated consistency checks. To address
domain anchoring, they develop five supplementary DSMLs derived from established industrial
standards (VDI/VDE 3682, OPC UA Robotics, IEC 61293, TCP/IP, ISO 10303).
    This work is instructive in two ways, and we align with it on both.
     First, it isolates the same diagnosis: approaches that are methodologically sound but not
anchored in domain-specific knowledge are of limited practical applicability. That is our Section 2
thesis, arrived at from a different direction. The evidence is not the analyst’s failing to be rigorous;
it is the absence of a knowledge substrate to be rigorous about.
   Second, and importantly for how we position OpenDomain, their evaluation identifies a residual
gap: while the base language addresses clear representation and traceability, “domain anchoring
through standards” is not yet addressed in the base, and requires the supplementary standard-
based languages to achieve. We read this as evidence that domain knowledge is not a property of
a modelling notation. Notation does not carry it. It has to be carried by the knowledge artifacts
themselves, which is a different mechanism from the one [3] pursues. The two are complementary:
OpenDomain supplies the evidence and derivation substrate; a DSML such as RFLT can supply
the notation into which the resulting domain model is projected.
    We differ in scope. [3] is a systems-engineering contribution for a specific class of industrial
system, with a fixed notation and a case-study evaluation. OpenDomain is notation-agnostic and
domain-agnostic, and its output is artifacts and gates rather than a diagram language.

4.4 Domain-driven design
Domain-Driven Design [4] supplies the strategic core – bounded contexts, the distinction between
a domain model and a data model, the imperative to put behaviour in the domain – and the
anemic-domain-model critique [7] supplies the diagnostic that behaviour living in services rather
than entities produces transaction scripts, not domain models.
    OpenDomain adopts the strategic core. It departs from DDD in one respect that matters here:
DDD presupposes a team with access to domain experts, and its practical failure mode under AI
assistance is the reverse of its thesis. Because a generative model produces class hierarchies cheaply
and fluently, an agent applied to an insufficiently understood domain will produce an anemic-
looking but behaviourally-empty model with high surface plausibility: many entities, aggregates,
and repositories, with the actual rules nowhere. DDD’s warning about anemic models is, in the
agentic setting, better stated as: the model is not anemic, it is deficient, and the deficiency is
invisible because the structure is present.
   This is the general form of the problem in Section 1.2: structure presence masks semantic
absence.

4.5 Specification by example, BDD and TDD
Specification by Example [10] establishes that concrete examples can serve as the authoritative
specification, and BDD formalises this into a human-readable-yet-executable form (Gherkin [25]).
TDD [11] applies the same discipline one level down, at unit granularity. xUnit Test Patterns [12]
supplies the taxonomy of test doubles and their correct application.
   These are adopted, with one significant qualification discussed in Section 9: BDD, TDD,
contract testing, property-based testing and model-based specification are different verification
mechanisms with different applicability conditions, and a framework that mandates one globally is
prescribing a style rather than engineering a guarantee.

4.6 The Minimum Viable Product and its limits
The MVP concept [13, 14] addresses a real and important problem: the coupling of scope to
learning. Ship the smallest thing that produces the most learning per unit of effort, and let the
feedback loop correct the plan. This is a significant advance on plan-driven sequential delivery.
    Its applicability conditions are, however, narrower than its general use. Section 8 analyses
these conditions and argues that they are violated in the OpenDomain target setting. Section 8 is
not a criticism of MVP; it is a scope decision, argued and recorded, in the spirit of the framework
itself.

4.8 Mapping from founding operational protocols
OpenDomain is operationalised through three protocol documents that function as its executable
specification: a domain-engineering protocol (two variants, a practical and a knowledge-driven), a
spec-driven-engineering protocol, and an opportunity/innovation-engineering protocol. These are
the material from which the framework is abstracted; Appendix D gives the full mapping. Two
design rules govern that abstraction and are stated here because they explain the framework’s
shape:

Rule A (do not invent). A protocol instruction that requires information the
agent may not possess must degrade to an explicit UNKNOWN or CONFLICTING
record, never to a plausible default.

Rule B (mechanise the prohibition). Any protocol instruction phrased as
"never do X" must be expressible as a check the tooling can execute, or it
will be honoured in practice only by authors who already honour it in
principle. Most of the prohibitions in the founding protocols are of the
unmechanised kind; Section 7 converts the tractable ones into gates.


5 THE OPENDOMAIN FRAMEWORK
5.1 The pipeline
OpenDomain organises engineering as a nine-gate pipeline. Each gate has a precondition (what
must hold to proceed) and produces artifacts consumed by the next.

Gate 1   context       Establish what the project is, for whom, and under
                       what constraints. No modification of code.
Gate 2   research      Acquire and register evidence; detect saturation and


                                                13
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                            5   THE OPENDOMAIN FRAMEWORK



                          conflicts. Terminate research explicitly.
Gate 3     domain         Actors, stakeholders, entities, events, processes,
                          rules, states, ownership, cross-domain relations.
Gate 4     requirements   Derive requirements through the evidence gate; classify
                          and challenge; non-functional requirements in the
                          same pass; establish a baseline.
Gate 5     opportunity    Reconstruct the current state, extract assumptions,
                          find contradictions, generate and attack concepts,
                          formulate hypotheses, design experiments, converge.
Gate 6     specification Behavioural specification, acceptance criteria,
                          error and boundary scenarios, contracts.
Gate 7     plan           Architecture, data model, interfaces, decisions, task
                          decomposition, dependency ordering.
Gate 8     implementation Incremental, vertical-slice, test-first, with drift
                          checks between increment and specification.
Gate 9     completion     Full alignment audit: knowledge -> requirement ->
                          specification -> plan -> code -> test, plus
                          simplification and cross-module audits.

    Gate 5 is placed after requirements rather than before domain for a reason worth stating,
since it is counter-intuitive. Opportunity analysis performed before the domain is understood
produces feature generation. Performed after requirements exist, it operates on a validated model
of current reality and can therefore distinguish “this is what the domain does” from “this is what
we believe could be different”. Ordering it after Gate 4 also means the framework does not require
a project to justify its existence through innovation, which matters for regulated and internal
systems whose scope is externally determined. Gate 5 may terminate in “no material opportunity
identified”, which is a legitimate and recorded outcome.

5.2 The epistemic lattice
Every artifact carries a knowledge-type value drawn from a closed set of seven:

EXPLICIT    Directly documented in an authoritative source: regulation,
            standard, official documentation, textbook, peer-reviewed
            publication, published process description.
TACIT       Held in human expertise or organisational practice but not
            documented. Real, load-bearing, and not transferable by
            reading.
HYBRID      Reconstructed by combining explicit sources with expert or
            operational reasoning. The reasoning must be recorded.
INFERRED    Derived by the agent from multiple evidence items. The
            derivation must be recorded.
ASSUMED     A temporary position adopted to permit progress. Must be
            recorded with its expiry condition.
UNKNOWN     Evidence insufficient. No answer recorded.
CONFLICTING Reliable sources disagree. Both positions recorded; a resolution
            status is required.

   Three properties are deliberate and consequential.
     First, the set is closed and has no default. A new artifact without a declared knowledge type
is invalid. This is what makes ignorance representable: the frame does not permit an artifact to
exist in an undeclared epistemic state, which is the state in which fabrication is invisible.
   Second, the lattice is not ordered in the way confidence usually is. TACIT is not “less” than
EXPLICIT; it is differently located, and often more decision- relevant, since undocumented
operational heuristics frequently govern what actually happens while the documented procedure
describes what is supposed to happen. The framework records both and marks the divergence
rather than resolving it silently. The related distinction between historically necessary and
fundamentally necessary steps is treated in Gate 5 as an assumption to be challenged, not as a
modelling decision to be made silently.

                                                  14
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                          5   THE OPENDOMAIN FRAMEWORK



    Third, epistemic state is per-artifact and mutable, and the lattice does not permit monotonic
promotion. An artifact may move INFERRED → EXPLICIT when a source is found, or ASSUMED
→ CONFLICTING when a contradicting source appears, or any state → UNKNOWN when a
source is retracted. Gate 8 detects the specific prohibited transition: promotion of ASSUMED
or INFERRED to VALIDATED without an intervening EXPLICIT or corroborating evidence.
This is the mechanisation of “epistemic laundering”, and it is, in our assessment, the single most
valuable check in the framework.

5.3 The evidence gate on derivation
The framework forbids the direct promotion of an external statement into a requirement. The
permitted path is:

Source
  -> Finding           what the source literally states
  -> Interpretation    what we read that to mean, in our situation
  -> Candidate Need    a need this might serve
  -> Requirement       a SHALL statement derived from a candidate need


    The value of the intermediate steps is not ceremonial. Consider the most common concrete
failure in agent-generated requirements: a competitor publishes a product page stating that its
inventory system supports “reservation”. The failure is the jump from that page to “REQ: The
system SHALL support stock reservation.”

Source        Competitor product page, retrieved DATE.
Finding       The page states that reservation is supported.
Interpretation The page does not state *why*, does not state whether
               reservation is legally required or commercially optional,
               and does not state the lifecycle position of reservation
               relative to payment.
Candidate     Possibly the deployment organisation experiences stock loss
              from concurrent allocation of the same units.
Requirement   Blocked. The candidate need is a hypothesis; it requires
              domain evidence or explicit business confirmation before it
              can become a requirement.


   The gate converts an illegitimate step into a visible, closable question. That is the entire
mechanism.

5.4 The traceability spine
OpenDomain maintains one ordered chain:

Source -> Evidence -> Domain Knowledge -> Requirement -> Use Case
  -> Specification -> Acceptance Criterion -> Contract -> Plan -> Task
  -> Code -> Test -> Validation Result


   over a fixed relation vocabulary of thirteen relations:

supports         A rests on evidential support from B
derived-from     A was extracted from B
implements       A realises B
depends-on       A cannot exist without B
constrains       A narrows the permitted implementations of B
verifies         A checks that B holds
supersedes       A replaces B
conflicts-with   A and B cannot both hold as written
affected-by      A is downstream of B and must be revisited when B changes


                                                15
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                             5   THE OPENDOMAIN FRAMEWORK



owned-by          A is the single owning domain of B's concept
contains          A subsumes B into its own lifecycle
answers           A answers B's question
tests             A tests B's falsifiable claim


    The relation vocabulary is closed. This matters: an open relation vocabulary degenerates
into a graph of undocumented semantics in which “derived-from” and “related-to” become
indistinguishable, and impact analysis becomes guesswork.
    One relation is reserved by construction. The binding edge from a verification artifact to the
acceptance criterion it checks is expressed as tests, not as verifies, because the direction of the
edge carries the epistemic claim: a test asserts a falsifiable proposition, whereas verifies would
assert that the proposition is already established. The provisioner emits that edge, and never
infers it (Section 10.8).
   The spine supports two query classes that define the framework’s operational value proposition:

Q-Why      For artifact A, traverse backwards along supports / derived-from /
           implements until a source with an authority rating is reached.
           The answer to "Why does this code exist?" is then a path, not a
           recollection.
Q-Blast    For artifact A, traverse forwards along implements / depends-on /
           affected-by / verifies to enumerate every artifact invalidated by a
           change to A.


   Both queries are graph traversals over a version-controlled file set. Both are answerable by
an agent without interpretation. This is, we argue, the property that most directly determines
whether an agent can maintain a large system coherently: not the quality of any single generation,
but the ability to enumerate the consequences of a change.

5.5 Bidirectionality and the pre-requirements question
Traceability literature distinguishes requirements traceability (forward, from requirement to
implementation) from pre-requirements traceability (backward, from requirement to the stakeholder
information, documentation, or legacy system that introduced it). The agentic setting makes the
backward direction the binding constraint, because it is exactly the direction in which an agent
generates content without provenance: the forward direction is recoverable from the artefacts
themselves, the backward direction is not.
    OpenDomain therefore treats the source registry as a first-class artifact with a mandatory
authority rating and access date, and treats a requirement without a resolvable evidence path
as a gate failure rather than as an incomplete document. Pre-requirements traceability is the
mechanism by which the framework distinguishes “the organisation needs this” from “a model
inferred it”.

5.6 Cross-domain analysis and concept ownership
For systems with more than one module, the framework requires that every significant shared
concept have exactly one owning bounded context, and that cross-context references be explicit
relationships rather than redefinitions. The specific failure prevented is concept fragmentation:
Sales.Customer, Inventory.Customer, Procurement.Customer existing as three incompatible records
for one real-world party.
   The framework also requires, for every capability, an explicit statement of what it produces,
consumes, reads, writes, triggers, and depends on, and requires that a new module never be
analysed in isolation. Ownership is recorded as an owned-by relation, making the single-owner
constraint checkable.

5.7 The opportunity stage
Gate 5 exists to prevent a specific and highly probable agent failure: given a competitor’s feature
list and a domain description, the agent will produce a feature list. This is feature replication,
and it is presented in the register of analysis.
    The stage’s structure is designed to make replication structurally visible:

 1. Current-state reconstruction. Represent how the problem is solved today as
    Actor -> Action -> Information -> Decision -> Action -> Outcome.
 2. Friction discovery. Search beyond stated feature requests: pain, friction,
    waste, delay, repetition, duplication, context switching, information
    asymmetry, decision bottlenecks, error-prone operations, invisible work,
    unmet needs users do not state because they assume them impossible.
 3. Assumption extraction. For each significant step, ask why it exists and
    whether the creating constraint still holds. Record ASSUMED artifacts.
 4. Contradiction analysis. Identify places where current behaviour and
    desired behaviour conflict, and the assumption underlying each.
 5. Opportunity spaces. Convert problems into spaces of possible direction,
    not into features.
 6. Divergent exploration. Apply a fixed operator set (remove, automate,
    delegate, merge, split, invert, predict, proact, personalise, autonomise,
    combine, abstract, reframe, reverse) and generate at least three
    materially different concepts, not three variants of one.
 7. Concept critique. Attack each concept on necessity, novelty, value,
    feasibility, evidence, risk, adoption, complexity, defensibility,
    unintended consequence, and failure mode.
 8. Hypothesis formulation. Convert the surviving concepts into falsifiable
    hypotheses with target population, metric, threshold, experiment, and
    failure condition.
 9. Experiment design. Prefer the cheapest meaningful experiment: interview,
    Wizard-of-Oz, synthetic-data test, technical spike, concierge, small
    vertical slice. This is the mechanism by which a candidate is killed
    before implementation.
10. Convergence. A concept is frozen only against an explicit checklist; the
    outcome is a Concept Decision Record stating why this concept is being
    pursued, not that it is the best concept.


    A named anti-pattern check (“innovation red flag”) is applied: if the proposal is substantially
a competitor feature list, justified by “everybody does this”, identifying no underlying problem,
challenging no assumption, exploring no alternative workflow, and proposing no new mechanism,
the stage returns to assumptions and contradictions rather than proceeding.
   Two outputs are explicitly recorded as legitimate: “no material opportunity identified”, and
“hypothesis failed; pivot recorded with the surviving knowledge”. Failure is retained as an artifact
rather than deleted, because deleted failures are re-derived.

5.8 Uncertainty as a managed, not suppressed, quantity
The framework maintains a first-class uncertainty registry. Every entry records the question, why
it is unknown, the evidence available, the possible answers, the impact of each, the confidence,
and the validation required. Two disciplines apply.
    The first is that uncertainty may be closed by research but not by preference. Where external
evidence can answer a question, the agent researches rather than asks. The agent asks the human
only when the decision is project-specific, when the answer cannot responsibly be researched, when
materially different interpretations have materially different consequences, or when authorisation
is required. When asking, the framework requires the agent to state the question, why it matters,
the known alternatives, the impact of each, and (on request) a recommendation. The intent is to
preserve the human’s scarce attention for the decisions only a human can make, which in practice
means business and organisational decisions, not vocabulary questions.

                                                 17
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                            5   THE OPENDOMAIN FRAMEWORK



    The second is that regional and organisational context is evidence-requiring. Generic inter-
national workflow is not a substitute for local practice; local regulation, terminology, payment
systems, administrative procedure, taxation, reporting requirements, and organisational structure
each require their own evidence.

5.9 Architecture is derived, not selected
Architecture follows the domain. The framework requires that architectural choices be justified
by domain boundaries, consistency requirements, scale, security, deployment constraints, data
ownership, team constraints, and existing technology – and explicitly not by fashion. Microservices,
event sourcing, CQRS, message brokers, plugin systems, generic frameworks, and distributed
caches are listed as constructs requiring a concrete justifying requirement. The default posture is
the simplest architecture that satisfies the validated requirements, which for many systems is a
modular monolith.
    The rationale is not conservatism; it is that each distributed construct transfers a solution (a
distributed transaction) into a distributed set of failure modes, and that transfer must be paid for
deliberately. A significant share of agent-generated architectures fails here, because distribution is
visually attractive in a generated dependency graph and its costs are not visible in one.

5.10 Brownfield: existing code is evidence, not specification
For existing systems the framework requires reverse specification with explicit behaviour classi-
fication. Observed behaviour is classified as documented, inferred, observed, legacy-accidental,
intentional, compatibility-preserving, or unknown. Nothing is deleted on the grounds of being
undocumented, and nothing is preserved on the grounds of being implemented. Classification
precedes change.
    This is the direct brownfield analogue of the evidence gate, and it prevents the common agent
error of treating the current implementation as the requirement.

5.11 The minimal-artifact principle
The framework enumerates document types generously and then requires that each be justified by
at least one of: clarity, traceability, verification, coordination, decision record, maintenance value.
Documents that provide none of these are to be merged or deleted. This is the counterweight
to the framework’s own proliferation: a method that generates forty documents for a two-week
change will be abandoned, and abandonment returns the process to the unstructured state it was
meant to replace.
   Where the reference implementation scaffolds a full document set, the scaffolding is a menu,
not a mandate.

5.12 The framework’s own audit
The framework is subject to its own requirements. This section states its epistemic status precisely,
because doing so is the framework’s first test of itself.
• The failure analysis (Section 1.2) is INFERRED from observed agent behaviour, not from a
  controlled measurement.
• The claim that structure presence masks semantic absence is INFERRED.
• The MVP rejection (Section 8) is an argument, not a measurement. The empirical claim is

    Two properties are load-bearing.
   Stable typed prefixes. Because the prefix encodes the kind, a link derived-from:
["RULE-001"] is checkable by inspection, and a requirement that cites a test is a distinct,
detectable condition from one that cites a rule. The alternative – opaque UUIDs with typed edges
– makes the most valuable class of error (a requirement citing a test as its justification) invisible.
   Scoped identifiers. Where a system has multiple bounded contexts, identifiers may be scoped
(REQ-INVENTORY-012, AC-INVENTORY-012-01), and acceptance criteria inherit the scope of their
requirement. This makes cross-context traceability and cross-context collision both detectable,
supporting the single-owner constraint of Section 5.6.

6.2 Artifact statuses
Thirteen statuses, in lifecycle order:

Draft, Researched, Supported, Validated, Approved, Implementing,
Implemented, Verified, Blocked, Open, Conflicting, Deprecated, Superseded


   The ordering is a partial order over epistemic and lifecycle state jointly, and the two are not
the same axis. VALIDATED is an epistemic claim; IMPLEMENTING is a lifecycle claim. The
framework’s core discipline is that an artifact may be IMPLEMENTED while its epistemic status
remains ASSUMED, and the gate set checks this specifically: implementation progress does not
launder epistemic state.

6.3 Artifact record structure
Every artifact is a Markdown document with a heading carrying the identifier and a fenced
TOML block carrying structured fields, followed by prose. The split matters: structured fields are
machine-checked; prose is human-read. The reference implementation parses the heading and the
block, and treats the prose as uninterpreted content, which means the framework does not require

natural language to be parseable – an important property given that one of its primary producers
is a language model.

6.4 Authority and confidence
Two orthogonal scalar attributes distinguish the strength of support from the familiarity of the
claim.

Authority    how binding the source is *for this particular claim*. A
             manufacturer is authoritative for its own product; a regulator is
             authoritative for compliance; a peer-reviewed paper is
             authoritative for a research claim; an experienced practitioner may
             be authoritative for tacit operational knowledge. Authority is
             claim-relative, not source-relative, and the framework records
             authority per evidence item rather than per source.

Confidence    HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW, applied to the inference, not to the source.
             Confidence is reduced, not preserved, when multiple derived
             sources rest on one original assertion; the framework explicitly
             refuses to inflate confidence from repetition.


7 THE NINE QUALITY GATES
7.1 Design constraint
Gates are pure functions from a workspace index to a report. They do not mutate state. Two
consequences follow. First, gate evaluation is reproducible: running it twice yields the same result,
which is what makes it trustworthy enough to block. Second, gates are auditable as code: the
exact condition that failed is nameable, which is what makes remediation possible. A gate that
both mutates and judges cannot be trusted in either role.
    Severity has three levels. BLOCKER prevents progression. WARNING requires an explicit
recorded decision to proceed. NOTE is advisory.

8 REJECTING THE MINIMUM VIABLE PRODUCT
8.1 Position
The framework does not adopt the Minimum Viable Product as a scope-governing device. This is
a deliberate, argued decision, recorded here in the format the framework itself requires, and it
is one of the points on which the framework most distinguishes itself from conventional product
process.

8.2 What MVP does well
The MVP addresses a genuine and important defect in plan-driven sequential delivery: scope is
committed before the feedback that would invalidate the commitment is available. Its contribution
is to make the learning-per-unit-effort ratio the governing quantity, and to bound the cost of being
wrong. This is correct, and the spiral model [13] anticipated it.

8.3 Why its preconditions fail here
MVP assumes, without exception, four preconditions.

P1. The problem is sufficiently known to define a deliverable at all. MVP
    scopes *a solution*; it presupposes that the solution shape is known well
    enough to cut.
P2. The unknown is market response, not domain semantics. MVP's learning
    variable is user reaction to a working artefact.
P3. The feedback loop is fast relative to the cost of a wrong assumption.
P4. Value can be delivered in a thin slice and accumulated.


   Each fails in the target setting, and the failures are not marginal.
    P1 fails directly. Section 2 establishes that the dominant failure mode is semantic fabrication:
the solution shape is precisely what is not known. An MVP cut from an unvalidated domain
model does not risk wasted effort; it fixes the fabrication as a baseline against which all subsequent
work is measured. This is worse than no MVP, because it converts an unknown into an apparently
settled premise.
    P2 fails. In the G2 setting – reconstruct a domain without access to experts – the unknown is
regulatory obligation, business rule, actor decision right, and jurisdictional convention. None of
these yield to user feedback, because users are not the authority on them and, importantly, users
frequently do not know that they are being violated. A user testing a compliant invoicing system
will report that it works.
    P3 fails. Domain validation latency is dominated by source discovery, expert availability, and
organisational decision, not by build time. In an AI-assisted regime build time approaches zero,
so the ratio inverts: the expensive step is now the one MVP does not shorten. Optimising the
quantity MVP optimises – learning per unit of engineering effort – becomes degenerate, because
engineering effort is no longer the binding constraint.
    P4 fails when the thin slice is not independently valuable. Many domain obligations are
irreducibly collective: a ledger entry is not useful without the double-entry relation; a stock
movement is not useful without the reservation that constrains it. Slicing these produces artefacts
that are small and useless, which is the worst of both worlds.

8.4 What replaces it
Three mechanisms replace MVP, at different granularities.



                                                 26
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering              8   REJECTING THE MINIMUM VIABLE PRODUCT



8.4.1 Evidence-bounded capability classification
Every candidate capability is classified by evidentiary basis, not by appeal:

CORE           Required by a validated rule or a regulatory constraint.
               Omitting it produces a defective or non-compliant system.
IMPORTANT      Supported by evidence, addresses a validated need, not
               required for correctness.
OPTIONAL       Plausible; evidence is weak; requires a decision.
FUTURE         Justified in principle; not justified now.
OUT_OF_SCOPE   Explicitly excluded, with the reason recorded.
EXPERIMENTAL   Exists to answer a question, not to deliver value. Marked
               non-production. (Section 8.4.3.)


    The classification is a function of the evidence state, not of delivery convenience. A capability
cannot be classified CORE without a resolvable derivation path to evidence. This is the essential
difference from MVP scoping: MVP asks “what is the smallest thing we can ship?”, this asks
“what does the evidence oblige us to build, and what is the evidence status of everything else?”.

8.4.2 The evidence-complete increment
The unit of delivery is a vertical slice that is complete in evidence, not thin in feature count:

  one business rule
+ its epistemic state and evidence path
+ its specification, with error and boundary scenarios
+ its acceptance criteria
+ its verification mechanism
+ its impact record against existing artifacts


    An increment is not complete because it compiles or because its tests pass. It is complete
when the chain rule → requirement → specification → acceptance criterion → test is closed and
each link is declared. This is larger than an MVP slice in the feature dimension, because it carries
the whole chain, and smaller in the risk dimension, because the unverified surface is bounded by
one rule rather than by an entire assumed product shape.
    The trade is deliberate. MVP buys speed with unresolved semantics; the evidence-complete
increment buys semantic closure at reduced parallelism. In a regime where code generation is
cheap, this is the correct direction of exchange and the framework is explicit that it is a trade, not
a free improvement.

8.4.3 MVP retained where it is actually valid: the experiment
MVP’s underlying technique – ship a thin artefact to a limited audience to obtain feedback quickly
– is excellent, and is retained inside Gate 5 under a different name and a different obligation.

The Wizard-of-Oz MVP becomes EXP-001, an experiment artifact.
Its purpose is to answer a stated question, not to deliver value.
Its scope is bounded and its non-production status is mandatory.
Its result is VALIDATED / PARTIALLY_VALIDATED / REJECTED / INCONCLUSIVE /
NEEDS_MORE_EVIDENCE / PIVOT.
An INCONCLUSIVE result may not be recorded as VALIDATED (Gate 5, G5-W3).
A validated capability still requires the full evidence chain before it
becomes a requirement; the experiment is evidence *about* the concept, not a
substitute for the requirement's derivation.


   This is the correct place for MVP thinking in the framework, and the reason for relocation is
precise: an experiment is a knowledge-production device, and the framework permits knowledge-
production devices only inside the stage whose purpose is knowledge production, with their outputs
subject to that stage’s falsification rules.

8.5 Consequences accepted
Rejecting MVP has costs, and the framework records them rather than rationalising them.

C-1    Slower time to first deployable output, because the first increment
       carries a full evidence chain.
C-2    Lower parallelism in early increments, because each touches the
       knowledge substrate.
C-3    Requires access to sources, or to a human able to make business
       decisions. Where neither exists, the framework will correctly report a
       high volume of UNKNOWN, and the project will appear blocked. It will be
       blocked: that is the accurate report.
C-4    Requires the discipline to record "no material opportunity" rather than
       proceeding, which is socially harder than proceeding.


   C-3 is the binding constraint and deserves emphasis. In the G2 setting, with no experts and
weak sources, the framework’s most likely output is a large uncertainty registry and a small set
of CORE requirements. This is a worse project plan and a better project. The framework’s
contribution is to make that distinction legible rather than to conceal it.

8.6 What would falsify this position
Consistent with the framework’s own epistemics, the position is stated with its refutation condition.
The MVP rejection is wrong if a controlled comparison (S2, Section 13) shows that MVP-scoped
systems achieve equal or better correctness against validated requirements, at lower cost, in a
setting where domain knowledge is not the binding constraint. We expect this to be true in
low-domain-entropy settings – pure UI work, well-specified algorithm implementation, greenfield
tools with no external obligations – and expect it to be false in the setting the framework targets.
The framework should therefore be applied selectively, by gate outcome, and its applicability is an
OPEN empirical question (RQ5).

9 THE TDD / BDD / SDD PROCESS
9.1 The error this section corrects
The TDD, BDD and SDD literatures are usually presented as a pipeline:

write specification (SDD) -> write feature files (BDD) -> write unit
tests (TDD)


      This is wrong in two ways, and both errors are costly in the agentic setting.
    The first error is sequence. The three are not successive phases; they are concentric loops of
different radii that run concurrently, each with its own artifact type, its own unit of verification,
and its own definition of done. A team that treats them as phases performs them in that order
and then maintains three sets of artifacts that describe the same behaviour at three levels of
redundancy, drifting apart.
    The second error is scope. Presented as a pipeline, SDD appears only at the front, as
documentation produced before implementation. In reality SDD is the outermost loop and it
is the only loop that continues after the code is written, because the specification remains the
authority against which drift is detected. A pipeline presentation cannot express this, and so
cannot express drift detection.

OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                               9   THE TDD / BDD / SDD PROCESS



9.2 The three loops, defined
Loop    Radius               Primary artifact        Unit of verification
----    ------------------   ---------------------- --------------------
SDD     system / feature     SPEC + AC (Markdown)    acceptance criterion,
                                                   reviewed by a human
BDD     component            .feature + step defs     scenario, executed
                                                   against a real boundary

                                                   no I/O

----    ----------------------   -------------------------------------------

                                 boundary scenarios exist; links to REQ resolve

        full stack               application boundary, not a mock of it

      Three properties of this table are load-bearing.
    First, the three definitions of done are different and none substitutes for another. “The unit
tests pass” is not “the scenario passes” is not “the acceptance criterion is reviewed”. Three teams
reporting “tests pass” may be reporting three different things, and the ambiguity is invisible
because the phrase is shared.
    Second, SDD has no test process. Its verification is human review. This is a weakness and it
is the reason the BDD loop exists: BDD is the mechanism that converts SDD’s reviewed prose
into something executable, which is why BDD’s value is proportional to how much specification
there is, not to how much code.
    Third, the unit of verification differs in kind, not only in size. An acceptance criterion is a claim
about observable behaviour at a boundary; a function contract is a claim about a transformation
in isolation. Substituting one for the other produces either untestable specifications or tautological
tests. Section 9.8 gives the detectable signatures.

9.3 The outer loop: SDD
Cycle time: one specification increment, typically hours to days. Artifacts: SPEC, AC, SCN. No
code, no test.
1. Select the AC set for this increment from a validated REQ.
2. Write the main scenario in observable terms: actor, boundary, state before, state after.
3. Write the alternative, error, and boundary scenarios. This step is not optional and is the single
   most-skipped part of specification practice in the field; Gate 6 (G6-B3) exists because of it.
4. Make each AC measurable. “The system SHALL be fast” fails; “the system SHALL return the
   allocation within 200 ms at p95 under 500 concurrent reservations“ passes.
5. Close Gate 6.
6. Hand the AC set to the BDD loop. The handoff is mechanical and is performed by the
   provisioner (Section 10.7), not by hand.
   The SDD loop does not close when the code works. It closes when the AC set is reviewed and
unambiguous, whether or not any code exists. Spec-first mode [2] terminates here.

9.4 The middle loop: BDD
Cycle time: one scenario, minutes. Artifacts: .feature, step definitions, World/harness.
1. The feature file is derived from the AC set, tagged with the AC id (Section 10.7). It is not
   hand-written, because a hand-written feature file has no guarantee of corresponding to any AC.

                                                   29
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                               9   THE TDD / BDD / SDD PROCESS



2. Step definitions bind each step to an application port. The generated World object exists to
   make this structurally enforced: step definitions receive a harness, never a database handle.
3. The scenario runs. On first run every step is a deliberate failure carrying the full provenance
   chain in its message. This is the loop’s red step, and it is the correct initial state.
4. Implement until green, at whatever level the behaviour requires.
5. Refactor the implementation; the scenario must not move.
   The constraint in step 2 is what earns BDD its cost. If step definitions may reach directly into
persistence, a green scenario proves nothing about the application’s behaviour; it proves that the
database contains a row. The indirection through a port is the whole justification for the layer.
   Corollary, stated because it is frequently ignored: BDD is the wrong level for a library with
no application boundary. There is no boundary to observe, so a scenario can only reach past it.
Section 10.13 makes the provisioner refuse in that case rather than generate hollow scenarios.

9.5 The inner loop: TDD
Cycle time: minutes. Artifacts: unit test, domain object, test double.
   Standard red-green-refactor [11], with two constraints specific to this framework.
    Constraint 1 – the unit test traces to a RULE, not to an AC. This is the detail most often
got wrong and it corrupts the traceability model when mistaken. The two levels have genuinely
different provenances:

RULE-004 --verifies-->    TDD unit test
AC-014-01 --verifies-->   BDD scenario
SPEC-014 --contains-->    both


   The unit test’s justification is the business rule it enforces. The scenario’s justification is
the acceptance criterion that describes the behaviour at the boundary. A unit test that cites
AC-014-01 is asserting that a level-2 specification is also a level-1 contract, which is false in general
and tempting always.
    Constraint 2 – the unit test runs with no I/O. Persistence, network and clock are injected. A
unit test that touches a database is not a unit test; it is a slow integration test that has lost the
ability to localise failure.

9.7 Rigour selection
BDD, TDD, contract testing, property-based testing, model-based specification and formal
verification are not stylistic alternatives. They are mechanisms with different applicability
conditions, different cost profiles, and different classes of defect they can detect. A framework
that mandates one globally prescribes taste and calls it engineering.
   OpenDomain therefore makes verification-rigour selection an engineering decision, made per
requirement against stated criteria, and recorded as an artifact.

Requirement character            Primary mechanism       Secondary
------------------------------   ---------------------   ------------------
Business behaviour, acceptance   BDD / Gherkin           Contract test
Unit-level contract              TDD                     Property-based test
REST interface                   OpenAPI + schema test    Pact
GraphQL interface                GraphQL SDL              Pact
gRPC interface                   Protocol Buffers        Pact
Event interface                  AsyncAPI                Consumer-driven test
Service compatibility            Pact / Specmatic        Integration
State-heavy lifecycle            State machine / model    Model-based
Mathematical invariant           Property-based           Formal (if tractable)
Performance envelope             Benchmark + load test    Synthetic data
Security property                Adversarial test         Static analysis
Uncertain / contested rule       Scenario, not test       Recorded as UNKNOWN


9.8 Three misuses and their detectable signatures
M1   BDD used for unit testing. Step definitions assert on internal
     structures; scenarios name data structures rather than domain states;
     a single class is named in most scenarios.
     Signature: a Given clause naming a data structure ("a row in stock")
     rather than a domain state ("an item with 100 on hand"). Checkable.


M2   TDD used as acceptance. Test names mirror requirement text; the suite is
     green while the behaviour at the boundary is absent or wrong.
     Signature: unit suite green and zero BDD scenarios exist for the AC set.
     Gate 8 (G8-B4) reports every AC as uncovered, which is the correct and
     sufficient detection.


M3   SDD used as documentation. Specification written or edited after the
     implementation, revised to match what the code does.
     Signature: a specification file and an implementation file changed in the
     same commit. This is mechanically detectable from version-control
     history and is a BLOCKER, added as G6-B6. The check is crude and
     deliberately so: it will occasionally fire on a legitimate correction,
     and the cost of that false positive is one review comment, against a
     real cost of silent documentation-after-the-fact.


9.9 The contested-rule row
The final row of the selection table is the one most often omitted. When a behaviour depends on
a rule whose epistemic status is ASSUMED or CONFLICTING, the correct verification artefact is
not a test but a scenario with the uncertainty attached. Writing a test asserts the rule. Writing a
scenario that names the rule as contested records that the behaviour is a decision.
   The mechanical consequence is a prohibition the framework does enforce: a test may not be
the justification of a requirement. Justification flows downward from evidence; verification flows
upward from code. A requirement whose only support is a test asserting it is a closed loop, and
Gate 8’s G8-B3 exists partly to make such loops visible.

9.10 Executable specifications
Where practical, specifications are executable: Gherkin features linked to step definitions, Ope-
nAPI documents validated against implementations, property-based tests for invariants true by
construction rather than by example. The framework prefers executable specification because it
makes specification and test the same artifact, removing one drift class.
    The framework’s caution is the one expressed in [2]: generated code is a proposal requiring
verification, not a derivation with guaranteed correctness. Executable specifications bind specifica-
tion to verification. They do not validate the specification’s correctness against the domain, which
is a Gate 4 concern and which no amount of green scenarios can discharge.

10.1 The provisioning problem
Given the process in Section 9, the tool must answer five questions on every project: which test
runner, which BDD framework, which contract framework, how step definitions are wired, and
how tests are linked to acceptance criteria.
      Left to an agent, each of the five is answered wrongly in a characteristic way.

Q1    Wrong runner. The agent installs a framework that does not match the
      project's build system, or a second runner beside the existing one, so
      that the project's tests and the framework's tests cannot see each other.
Q2    Global install. The agent installs a tool globally. The toolchain stops
      being reproducible and the project stops being self-describing -- the
      exact failure the artifact-substrate stance exists to prevent.
Q3    Hand-written step definitions. The agent writes step definitions that
      reach past the application boundary into the database, producing green
      scenarios that prove nothing.
Q4    Unlinked tests. The agent writes tests that cite no AC. This is the
      catastrophic one: it breaks the chain on which the entire framework's
      value depends. Tests that cannot be traced to criteria are exactly as
      untraceable as requirements that cannot be traced to evidence, and the
      framework would then be silent about the most important class of defect
      it exists to detect.
Q5    Drifted feature files. Feature files are authored once and never
      re-derived, so the specification and the executable specification
      describe different systems.

    None of these are agent failures in the sense of carelessness. They are consequences of leaving
a structural decision unstructured.

10.2 Six provisioning principles
PP1    Detect, do not ask. The stack is mechanically detectable. Asking a user
       which test runner they use is a question the tool can answer, and
       Section 5.8 reserves human attention for decisions only a human can
       make.

PP2    Never global. Project-local manifests only, exact version pins. A
       globally installed test framework makes the project's verification
       unreproducible from the repository, which contradicts the framework's
       position that the artifacts must survive the tool.

PP3    Idempotent and declared. Provisioning state lives in od.toml.
       Re-running converges to a fixed point and produces no diff. A
       provisioning command that is not idempotent is not safe to put in a
       setup script, and will not be run.


                                                  32
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering   10   AUTOMATIC VERIFICATION TOOLCHAIN PROVISIONING



PP4   Derive structure, never author behaviour. The provisioner generates
      files, tags, empty step-definition stubs that fail, type stubs, and
      contract skeletons. It never generates a passing assertion. This is a
      safety property, not a limitation: an agent asked to "make the tests
      pass" must be structurally unable to comply by writing assertions, and
      must instead read the specification and implement the behaviour. Without
      this property, "add BDD and make it green" is a request the agent can
      satisfy without ever reading the specification, which defeats the
      framework's entire purpose.

PP5   Traceability is derived, therefore drift-free. Scenario tags are
      generated from the AC id, and re-synchronisation re-derives them from
      the specification. There is no path by which a scenario silently
      detaches from its criterion except by editing a generated tag, which is
      a visible diff in a generated file.

PP6   Provisioning is subject to the same anti-overengineering discipline as
      any other construct. Section 10.13 defines the refusal conditions, and
      refusal produces a recorded decision rather than silence.

10.5 The observation that makes this cheap
Every BDD framework in the table above is a port of Cucumber, and every one runs on its
language’s native test runner. Two consequences, both of which matter for the framework’s cost
case.
    First, step definitions and unit tests share one process, one mocking library, one coverage
tool, and one debugger. The BDD/TDD boundary is therefore not an operational wall: the same
fixtures, the same test doubles, the same CI job. The indirection costs indirection, not a second
toolchain.
   Second, Gherkin is the portable part of the artifact. Generated feature files are portable across
the entire column; only step definitions are language-bound. A project that changes language
keeps its executable specifications. This is a small instance of a general principle – the specification
outlives the tooling – and it is not obvious before it is noticed.

10.6 P2 – installation
1. Resolve the project’s own package manager. Never a second one.
2. Add the pinned dependency through that package manager, so that the lock file is updated by
   the tool that owns it.
3. Write the decision to od.toml AND emit a ProvisioningDecision artifact (kind DEC) record-
   ing: stack detected, frameworks chosen, rules R1-R4 applied, versions pinned, and the epistemic
   status of the detection.
4. Converge: a second run produces no diff.
5. On failure – no network, unavailable registry, version conflict – provisioning FAILS LOUDLY,
   writes nothing partial, and records the unavailability as a limitation. It must not emit a
   manifest that cannot resolve, and it must not silently proceed without verification.
    Step 3 deserves emphasis. An installation decision is a decision, and the framework records
decisions (Section 5.9 forbids hiding architectural decisions in code). A provisioner that silently
picks a BDD framework is making an undocumented architectural decision on the project’s behalf
– the exact anti-pattern the framework criticises elsewhere. Every automatic choice becomes a
reviewable artifact.

  #[given("an item with {on_hand:d} on hand and {reserved:d} already reserved")]
  fn given_stock(on_hand: i64, reserved: i64, _w: &mut World)

    Three properties. The body is todo! (or NotImplementedError, or pytest.fail) – a
deliberate failure, never a plausible-looking assertion. The failure message carries the full derivation
chain, so that whoever runs the suite first is told where the truth lives rather than what the string
says. And the step receives a World carrying a harness, never a database handle, which is what
makes the green scenario meaningful (Section 9.4).

10.8 P4 – traceability binding, and what it buys
Every generated scenario carries a tag per criterion and a provenance
comment:

@AC-INVENTORY-014-01 @SPEC-INVENTORY-014
# derives-from: RULE-004, REQ-INVENTORY-014, EVD-001
Scenario: Reject an over-reservation that exceeds available quantity
  Given an item with 100 on hand and 30 already reserved
  And a request to reserve 80
  When the reservation is requested
  Then the reservation is rejected
  And the allocatable quantity remains 70


   At audit time the provisioner parses tags, builds TEST- artifacts keyed by AC id, executes
the suite, records the per-criterion result, and writes the edges AC –verifies–> TEST and TEST
–verifies–> implementation.
   Four consequences, which are the actual payoff:

(a) G8-B4 becomes a measurement rather than a declaration. "An acceptance
    criterion has no test" is detected by the absence of a tagged scenario;
    and a tagged scenario that is *red* is a stronger finding still, because
    it means the specification and the code disagree right now.

(b) Regeneration cannot drift. Change the AC, run `od provision --sync`, the
    tag follows. Detachment requires editing a generated file.

OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering   10   AUTOMATIC VERIFICATION TOOLCHAIN PROVISIONING



(c) The framework can answer a question no static artifact set can: not "is
    this requirement implemented" but "is this requirement implemented *and
    does its acceptance criterion currently hold in the running system*". That
    is a dynamic property, and it is the closest the framework comes to the
    empirical validation of Section 5.12's G3.

(d) Test coverage becomes a query over the graph rather than a report
    someone regenerates.


10.9 Verification depth – the necessary caveat
The binding above is only as strong as the suite’s ability to run. A project whose scenarios
require external services may not be executable at audit time. The framework therefore records a
verification-depth on every TEST- artifact and in every audit report:

executed     the scenario was run and its result observed now
discovered   the scenario exists and is bound; it was not run
declared     a link is asserted in an artifact; nothing was observed


    Audit output always states which depth produced it. A project reporting discovered coverage
has not demonstrated that anything works, and the framework must not present it as though it
had. This is the false-confidence error of Section 4.2, caught at the point where it is easiest to
catch – in the reporting layer, where coverage numbers are most often produced and least often
qualified.

10.10 P5 – unit-level binding, and binding provenance
The unit level needs its own binding mechanism, and it needs an honest account of how good that
mechanism is.

Method           Mechanism                             Binding Confidence
--------------   ------------------------------------ ------- ---------
ANNOTATED        `// od:RULE-004` comment in the test  explicit HIGH
ATTRIBUTED       language-native marker attribute      explicit HIGH
INFERRED         naming convention matched to glossary inferred LOW


    ANNOTATED via comment is the default because it requires no build-system change, works
in every language, and survives refactoring of the test harness. The convention fallback exists
because a large existing test suite will not be annotated, and a traceability graph with no unit-level
edges is much less useful than one with honestly-labelled inferred edges.
    This forces a small but important extension to the artifact model. The verifies relation
carries a binding attribute – GENERATED, ANNOTATED, or INFERRED – and the framework
never presents the three as equivalent. A link derived by matching a function name against a
glossary term is a hypothesis about intent, and marking it as though it were a generated tag
would be epistemic laundering performed by the framework itself.

10.11 P6 – contract testing
Where a specification carries a CTR artifact with a machine-readable source (OpenAPI, AsyncAPI,
GraphQL SDL, Protocol Buffers), the provisioner additionally generates:
• a provider verification test skeleton derived from the document: every operation, every required
  field, every documented status code, the authorisation requirement;
• a consumer contract derived from the consumer’s actual usage, if the consumer is in the
  workspace;
• CI registration of the pact verification step;

OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering   10   AUTOMATIC VERIFICATION TOOLCHAIN PROVISIONING



• a breaking-change check, which is the highest-value and cheapest item here:

git show HEAD~1:openapi.yaml | od contract diff

which fails on any breaking change. Removing a response field or widening a
constraint is a breaking change, and in agent-assisted development this
is a class of regression that no unit test catches because the tests are
regenerated with the code.

10.12 Brownfield: extract, do not install
The most common real case, and the one where restraint matters most.
1. Install nothing. The project already has a toolchain and a team’s accumulated choices; changing
   it is a cost with no benefit.
2. Walk the existing test tree. Extract from tags, comments, naming, and test metadata.
3. Bind by ANNOTATED where possible; fall back to INFERRED and mark confidence LOW.
4. Report the binding rate as the project’s traceability baseline.
5. Gate nothing on it in the first pass.
    Step 5 is a deliberate exception to the framework’s own gate discipline, and the exception is
justified. A project with a 20% inferred binding rate has just been told something about itself
that was previously unknown, and failing its build for its history is not the useful response. The
useful response is to make the 20% visible, agree it, and raise it incrementally. The framework’s
job here is to measure what was invisible, not to enforce a standard retroactively.
   This number is a deliverable. A project that learns its traceability baseline is 20% and that
60% of its tests cannot be connected to any acceptance criterion has received genuine information
that no amount of green CI would have given it.

10.13 The provisioning gate
Provisioning is refused, with the decision and reason recorded, when:
• fewer than the configured minimum behavioural acceptance criteria exist (default 3). BDD for
  one scenario is indirection with no payoff.
• no use case with a named actor exists (Section 9.4’s corollary).
• the change is marked EXPERIMENTAL and non-production.
• the project is a library with no application boundary.
• the project has declared the BDD-off profile.
   Refusal must emit a decision record naming the alternative mechanism – a naming-convention
binding, or scenario artifacts without execution. A refusal that produces silence is indistinguishable
from an oversight.
   The BDD-off profile deserves separate statement, because a framework that mandates a style
commits the error it criticises in Section 4.7. The profile is a first-class, declared mode in which the
team accepts the indirection cost and prefers acceptance-criterion identifiers carried in test names.
The framework supports it completely, and reports its coverage honestly at the correspondingly
lower verification depth. Declaring the profile is a decision record; using it is not a violation.

10.14 Limits and failure modes
Stated plainly, because this subsystem is where the framework is most likely to disappoint.

F1   Provisioning produces a red suite. Correct by design and by PP4, but a
     team that runs the provisioner, sees N failures, and has not read the
     specification will conclude the tool is broken. Mitigation: the
     provisioner reports "N scenarios generated, all red by design" as a
     first-class result, never as a project failure.


                                                  37
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering    10   AUTOMATIC VERIFICATION TOOLCHAIN PROVISIONING



F2    Cucumber-family step registries are process-global. Two bounded contexts
      defining "an item with X" collide at runtime, and the failure appears far
      from its cause. Mitigation: generated step definitions are namespaced by
      scope and the generator rejects ambiguous definitions. This is the most
      frequently hit limitation in practice.

F3    BDD carries real indirection cost. Teams that find it painful will drop
      it, and history suggests many do. Mitigation: the BDD-off profile. We
      regard this as a genuine risk to adoption of the whole framework, not
      only of this subsystem.

F4    Version coupling. The BDD framework, the native runner, and the language
      version move together. Pinning controls this but does not remove it, and
      upgrades require re-verification -- a maintenance cost the framework
      imposes and must justify (Section 17.3).

F5    THE MOST IMPORTANT LIMIT. Auto-provisioning improves the *chain*, not the
      *content*. If the acceptance criterion is vague, the generated tag is
      precise and the generated scenario faithfully encodes the vagueness. The
      provisioner can make an unspecified criterion consistent, tagged, and
      permanently green. It cannot make it specified. Provisioning must never
      be allowed to create the impression that a verified chain validates its
      own inputs, because a chain of tagged, well-formed, green scenarios over
      a vague criterion is the most reassuring artefact the framework can
      produce and it means nothing. Gates 4 and 6 remain the only defences, and
      they operate on content, not on tags.

F6    Supply-chain exposure. Automatic installation of test frameworks is an
      attack surface, and a compromised test dependency executes with the
      project's privileges. Mitigation: exact version pins, recorded in the
      decision artifact, no global installation, and an installation diff that
      is reviewable in the same commit as everything else.

F7    The provisioner makes decisions. Every automatic choice is a decision and
      is recorded as one (Section 5.9). Without this the subsystem would be
      the largest single source of undocumented architectural decisions in the
      framework, which is intolerable.

    # G8-B2 laundering check
    # G8-B4 every AC has a scenario
    # G8-B7 provisioning drift: tag set == AC set
  od audit --verification executed
  od trace --why REQ-INVENTORY-014
      # REQ-014 <- EVD-001 <- SRC-001 (authority: HIGH)
  od impact AC-INVENTORY-014-01

   Two properties of this sequence are worth naming, because they are what the provisioning
subsystem buys.
   The BDD scenario is red before any unit work begins, and stays red until the boundary
behaviour genuinely exists. That is the TDD red step applied at the scenario level, and it is what
makes the three loops one process rather than three: no loop can be satisfied by a different loop’s
work.
    And the AC-to-test link was never written by anyone. It was generated from the specification
id. The chain REQ ← EVD ← SRC closes at Gate 9 not because a disciplined author maintained
a matrix, but because the artifacts were derived from one another.

11 REFERENCE IMPLEMENTATION ARCHITECTURE
11.1 Purpose and stance
The reference implementation (opendomain) exists to make the gates executable and the queries
answerable. It is deliberately not an IDE, not a requirements management system, and not a
code generator. Its entire function is to make the framework’s prohibitions checkable and its
traceability queries mechanical.
    Design stance: the artifact store is plain Markdown with fenced structured blocks, held in
the repository. This is a strong decision with three justifications. It is version-controllable and
therefore diffable. It is agent-editable without a tool. And it survives the tool: the knowledge
substrate does not become inaccessible if the framework is abandoned, which is the principal
failure mode of tool-coupled process.

    The provision module is the only component permitted to execute a package manager, and it
is the only component whose effects are not confined to the workspace’s own artifacts. Its purity

lockfile, and those two files are the workspace state it may touch. It may not modify application
source. G8-B7 exists because this is the one place where the framework’s automatic behaviour
changes the project’s build, and that change must be visible in review.

11.3 The purity constraint
The central implementation constraint, stated in the core crate’s own documentation, is that
every operation is a pure function of the workspace on disk. od audit and od gate never mutate.
Running them twice gives the same answer.
   This constraint is not stylistic. A checker that mutates cannot be trusted to judge, because its
judgement alters what it judges, and re-running it yields a different verdict. Reproducibility is
what allows a gate to be used as a blocking condition in an automated pipeline and what allows
two engineers to disagree productively about a finding rather than about a version.

11.4 Determinism
Because Q-Blast is a blocking condition at Gate 9 (G9-B5), traversal order must be specified, not
inherited. All iteration over sets and maps in impact analysis is performed in a canonical order
derived from identifier kind and identifier value. This is the only place in the implementation
where an unspecified detail is a correctness defect rather than a style issue, and it is called out
here because it is easy to introduce and hard to diagnose.

11.5 Extension points and their limits
The framework is notation-agnostic. Where a project requires SysML or a domain-specific
modelling language, a projection exports artifacts into the target notation; artifacts remain the
source of truth. Compatibility with the DSML approach of [3] is therefore complementary rather
than competing: the knowledge and its provenance live in artifacts, and the notation is a view.

13 EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Four studies. S1 and S2 are primary; S3 and S4 are supporting.

13.1 S1 – Longitudinal case study (addresses RQ1, RQ2, RQ6)
Design: single-case, embedded, longitudinal [24]. Unit of analysis: the change set. One greenfield
project and one brownfield project, 3-6 months, minimum 60 change sets.
      Data: every change set classified on four measures.

M1 Provenance completeness. Proportion of introduced requirements and domain
   rules with a resolvable evidence path. Ground truth established by
   independent review by a domain-experienced practitioner, blind to the
   framework's classification, to avoid circularity.
M2 Epistemic accuracy. Agreement between the framework's declared epistemic
   state and the independent practitioner's judgement. Measured as a
   confusion matrix over the seven values, reported in full.
M3 Unfounded-requirement rate. Requirements whose derivation path terminates
   at a competitor or at agent prior, as determined by review.
M4 Overhead. Wall-clock and token cost attributable to the framework, measured
   as the difference between framework-assisted and unassisted work on matched
   change sets.


   Analysis: within-case time series with change set as unit; intervention points marked at each
framework version change, to separate learning effects from tool effects. This matters: the first
weeks of any new process show inflated cost and inflated compliance, and attributing either to the
framework is a standard error.
   Threats: single-case generalisability; the reviewing practitioner’s own bias; the strong likelihood
that novelty effects inflate early compliance. Mitigated but not eliminated; see Section 14.

13.2 S2 – Controlled comparison against MVP scoping (addresses RQ5)
Design: within-subject crossover, alternating blocks. Teams or individuals implement matched
change sets under three regimes:

R-A    Evidence-complete increments (Section 8.4.2)
R-B    MVP scoping (thin slices, learning-per-effort optimisation)
R-C    Unstructured AI-assisted development (baseline)


      Outcome measures, in order of importance:

O1 Correctness against independently validated domain requirements. Not
   self-reported test pass rate. The requirements are validated by the
   practitioner before implementation begins and are not shown to
   implementers.
O2 Regret. Changes required post-deployment, weighted by the domain
   criticality of the violated rule.
O3 Cost. Total engineering time and token cost.
O4 Traceability query latency and correctness for Q-Why and Q-Blast.
O5 Change failure rate.


   The primary endpoint is O1, and specifically O1 measured against externally validated require-
ments. This is the design’s central commitment: the evaluation must not accept the system’s
own specification as the measure of the system’s correctness, which is the false-confidence error
identified in [2] and reproduced here as a measurement-design constraint rather than merely a
warning.
    Prediction: R-A will show higher O1 and lower O2, and worse O3 in the first increment,
converging over time. R-C will show the highest O2. A result in which R-B dominates on O1
falsifies Section 8 and is reported as such.

OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                                    14   THREATS TO VALIDITY



13.3 S3 – Epistemic-state audit (addresses RQ1, RQ3)
Design: mechanical. For every project adopting the framework, extract the epistemic state
transition history of every artifact and classify each transition.
    Measures: distribution of transition types; rate of ASSUMED/INFERRED → VALIDATED
transitions accompanied by a new evidence item (the laundering rate, the framework’s key metric);
time-to-close for artifacts in UNKNOWN and CONFLICTING; and the fraction of shipped-path
artifacts that were never promoted past their initial epistemic state.
    The last measure is the framework’s most uncomfortable and most informative: a high fraction
of never-promoted artifacts in a shipped path means the process produced accurate records of its
own ignorance and shipped anyway, which is a legitimate outcome under uncertainty and a failure
under compliance. Reporting it as a single number would be misleading; it is reported conditional
on the project’s compliance obligations.

13.4 S4 – Gate utility (addresses RQ3, RQ6)
Design: adversarial. Construct a corpus of workspaces seeded with known defects, one per gate
condition, plus controls. Measure per gate: true-positive rate, false-positive rate, and the time for
a competent engineer to reach a correct disposition on a BLOCKER finding.
    The false-positive measurement is the important one. A gate set that blocks on defects that
are not defects is a gate set that will be disabled, and a disabled gate set is worse than no gate
set because it creates the appearance of rigour. A target of under 5% false-positive rate per gate
condition is stated in advance; exceeding it is treated as a design defect requiring revision, not as
noise.

13.5 Instrumentation
Every gate execution emits a machine-readable report. The reports form the telemetry for S3 and
S4 with no additional instrumentation, which is a deliberate property of the purity constraint in
Section 11.3: a non-mutating checker produces observational data as a by-product of doing its job.

14 THREATS TO VALIDITY
14.1 Threats to construct validity
• M2 (epistemic accuracy) uses a human practitioner’s judgement as ground truth, and that
  judgement is itself fallible and may share biases with the framework’s categories. Mitigated by
  blind independent review and full confusion-matrix reporting, but not eliminated.
• M4 (overhead) attributes cost to the framework when part of the cost is learning the tool.
  Mitigated by the crossover design in S2 and by reporting overhead as a time series rather than
  a mean.
• The gate set may be complete for the failure modes this paper identifies and incomplete for
  others. S4 tests only the seeded corpus; it cannot establish coverage of unknown defect classes.

14.2 Threats to internal validity
• Novelty and training effects are the dominant confound in any process intervention. A framework
  that is being used for the first time will show inflated compliance and inflated cost. Mitigated
  by blocked crossover and time-series analysis; never fully eliminated in a field setting.
• Self-reported behavioural measures are subject to social desirability, since the framework makes
  adherence publicly visible in the repository. Mitigated by relying preferentially on mechanical
  measures (M1, M3, and all of S3/S4) over self-report.
• Selection effects: projects that adopt the framework are likely to differ systematically from
  those that do not. Not addressed by the protocol; stated as a limitation on external validity.

14.3 Threats to external validity
• The domain-entropy assumption. The framework’s advantage is predicted to increase with
  domain entropy – the degree to which correct behaviour depends on knowledge external to
  the codebase. For low-entropy work (UI, algorithms with given specifications) it may impose
  cost for no benefit. This is predicted, not measured, and is the most likely boundary of the
  framework’s applicability.
• Single-region, single-sector evidence. If S1 is conducted in one regulatory and organisational
  context, the framework’s regional-context discipline (Section 5.8) is untested.
• The framework has not been evaluated with a domain expert in the loop, which is the setting
  in which its authors expect the best cost-benefit ratio. The evaluation setting is therefore the
  harder case, which strengthens but does not replace the missing measurement.

14.4 Threats to conclusion validity
• Multiple endpoints across S1-S4 with no pre-registered primary endpoint invites selective
  reporting. Mitigation: O1 in S2 is designated primary in advance and the remaining measures
  are declared secondary here.
• The framework’s author is also its evaluator. Full mitigation requires independent replication,
  which is stated as future work (Section 18) rather than claimed.

15 DISCUSSION
15.1 What the framework changes about the agent’s role
The framework does not make the agent more capable of generating code. It makes the agent’s
assertions checkable.
   Concretely: the agent’s fluent output is admitted into the knowledge substrate only through a
path that requires it to declare what it is doing – extracting from a source, inferring, assuming, or
not knowing. An agent that says “I don’t know” can proceed, because UNKNOWN is a valid state
that blocks a specific gate and is therefore actionable. An agent that cannot say “I don’t know”
can only proceed by asserting, and its assertions are now checkable against declared evidence.
    This reframes the alignment question. The concern is usually expressed as “the model will
hallucinate”. The framework’s contribution is more specific: it makes the epistemic status of every
claim in the delivered system an inspectable property, so the question becomes not “did the model
hallucinate” but “which claims are unsupported, and do we know which ones those are”.

15.2 The framework as a research instrument
A secondary consequence is that adopting OpenDomain produces data about AI-assisted engineer-
ing that is otherwise unavailable. The artifact substrate records, per claim, the epistemic status
at which it entered, the evidence cited, and the transitions it underwent. This yields a dataset on
the distribution of epistemic states in generated requirements, which Section 12 notes is currently
UNKNOWN and which would be a contribution independent of the framework’s own effectiveness.

15.3 The cost objection, taken seriously
The most credible objection to this framework is cost. Every evidence record, uncertainty entry,
decision record, and gate check is overhead, and overhead competes with delivery. Three responses,
stated without rhetorical embellishment.
    First, the cost is front-loaded and the benefit is back-loaded, which is the worst possible shape
for adoption under deadline pressure and the best possible shape for correctness over a system’s



                                                 45
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                                              16   LIMITATIONS



life. In the AI-assisted regime, the asymmetry widens: generation is cheap, so the relative cost of
the knowledge substrate falls even as its absolute value rises.
    Second, the minimal-artifact principle bounds the cost directly. The framework does not require
forty documents; it requires that any document be justified. The reference implementation’s
forty-five template files are a menu.
   Third, and most honestly: the framework’s cost is highest exactly where it is most needed. In
the low-entropy setting it is overhead. This is a real cost, it is predicted rather than measured,
and Section 8.6 states the condition under which the framework’s central position is wrong.

15.4 On rejecting MVP
The MVP rejection is the framework’s most contestable position and deserves to be stated as an
argument a reader can reject.
    The argument is not that MVP is wrong. It is that MVP presupposes a solved domain, and
the framework’s target setting is defined by an unsolved domain. In a setting where the problem
is sufficiently known and the unknown is user response, MVP is the correct instrument and this
framework is overhead. In a setting where the unknown is what the rules are, MVP’s first cut is a
commitment to a fabrication, and the commitment is rewarded by every subsequent process step.
The framework declines to make that commitment and pays for the refusal in traceability work.
     Whether that refusal pays is RQ5, and it is open.

15.5 The three-stage separation
The framework preserves a separation the source protocols insisted on and that this paper
formalises:

DISCOVER     What is true of the world?
CHALLENGE    What could be better, and what would falsify that belief?
SPECIFY      What exactly must the software do?
IMPLEMENT    Realise the specification.
VALIDATE     Demonstrate alignment, and audit the alignment itself.


    The failure mode this guards against is the collapse of DISCOVER into SPECIFY, which
in an agentic setting is the default, because an agent asked “what should this system do?” will
answer, confidently, without having determined what is true. The framework makes that answer
inadmissible as a first act.

17 ETHICS AND SUSTAINABILITY
17.1 Epistemic honesty as an engineering duty
The framework’s central ethical claim is narrow and defensible: in domains governed by regulation,
safety, or fiduciary obligation, a system whose assertions are unfounded is a harm mechanism, and
the profession’s obligation extends to making the unfoundedness visible rather than to producing
well-documented systems whose documentation does not indicate its own reliability.
   This is a claim about legibility, not about correctness. The framework does not make software
more correct in the first instance; it makes incorrectness identifiable. In a regulated setting this is
the difference between a defect and an unknowable defect.

17.2 The risk of the framework being misused
• Ceremonial adoption. Producing the artifacts without performing the thinking. This is the
  failure mode of every process framework and is worsened by automation, which makes the
  artifacts cheap. Mitigation: the epistemic accuracy measure M2 in S1, which detects precisely
  this, since artifacts that are wrong about their own epistemic state are worse than absent
  artifacts.

                                                  47
OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                                             18   FUTURE WORK



• Weaponised uncertainty. Marking a required capability OUT_OF_SCOPE or UNKNOWN
  to avoid building it. Mitigation: the classification of Section 8.4.1 makes CORE require a
  derivation path, so deferral is visible as deferral.
• Manufactured authority. Citing a source that does not support the claim. Mitigation: G2-B2
  requires the source to be rated authoritative *for the claim*, which requires a per-claim
  judgement that is difficult to fake at scale and becomes visible in review.

17.3 Sustainability of the knowledge substrate
The artifacts are plain text, in the repository, under the project’s own licence. The knowledge
outlives the tool. This is a deliberate design choice with an environmental and professional
dimension: a process that requires a proprietary tool to maintain its own records will not be
maintained, and the records will be lost, which is a worse outcome than never having created
them.
   The framework also has a resource dimension worth stating. The most expensive resource it
consumes is expert attention, and the discipline of Section 5.8 is directed at that expense: ask the
human only for decisions that require a human. A framework that routes vocabulary questions to
domain experts has consumed the scarcest input in the system and produced nothing.

19 CONCLUSION
AI coding assistants have made the production of code cheap. They have not made the determi-
nation of what to produce any easier, and by lowering the cost of the first they have raised the
expected cost of the second: a wrong system is now cheap to build and expensive to discover.
    This paper has argued that the binding problem is not that models fabricate, which they
do, but that fabrication is indistinguishable from competence in the output, because the output
carries no record of how any of it is known. The proposed remedy is not a better prompt or a
longer process document. It is a representational change: make epistemic state part of the data,
attach provenance to every claim, and make the resulting structure checkable by a pure function
over a version-controlled workspace.
     OpenDomain makes that change in four commitments. The epistemic lattice, which permits
ignorance to be recorded and forbids it from being implicit. The evidence gate, which forbids an
external statement from becoming a requirement without passing through finding, interpretation,
and candidate need. The traceability spine, which makes “why does this exist?” and “what breaks
if this changes?” graph traversals rather than recollections. And the nine gates, which convert the
tractable prohibitions of a written method into checks that block.
   It adds two commitments that are less conventional. An opportunity stage that makes
innovation a falsifiable, kill-criteria-governed engineering process rather than a feature-list exercise.
And a formal rejection of the Minimum Viable Product as a scope-governing device, on the grounds
that its preconditions suppose a solved domain, which is precisely what the target setting lacks –
with the honest note that the empirical claim is open, that the argument is refutable, and that in
low-domain-entropy settings the framework is likely to be overhead.
    None of this has been shown to work. The reference implementation does not currently build.
The failure analysis is inferred from observation rather than measurement, and the effectiveness
claims are OPEN. We state this because the framework’s central claim is that unfounded assertion
is the primary hazard, and a paper making unfounded effectiveness claims would fail its own first
test. What is offered here is a falsifiable design, a precise specification, an evaluation protocol,
and an honest inventory of what remains unknown.

B.2 A note on gate ordering
G5 (opportunity) follows G4 (requirements) rather than preceding it. The alternative order –
opportunity before requirements – was considered and rejected: opportunity analysis conducted
against an unvalidated domain model produces concepts that are attractive and unfounded, and
the framework has no mechanism to detect that except the absence of the domain model itself.
   The chosen order has a cost: projects that are genuinely exploratory must establish a require-
ment baseline before they are permitted to explore. The mitigation is that G4’s classification
explicitly includes OUT_OF_SCOPE, and a project may baseline “the current state” as its
requirement set and proceed. This is a real cost, recorded rather than concealed.

C Appendix C – WORKED MICRO-EXAMPLE
C.1 The scenario
An assistant is asked: “add stock reservation to our inventory module so we stop overselling.”

C.2 Gate 2 (research) – what the sources say
SRC-001   Warehouse management reference documentation.
          Authority: HIGH for generic WMS workflow.
          Status: active.
SRC-002   The organisation's own purchase procedure (internal).
          Authority: HIGH for this organisation's receiving rules.
SRC-003   A competitor's product page stating "reservation supported".
          Authority: MEDIUM for market practice; LOW for this
          organisation's requirements.

EVD-001   Claim: Warehouse systems commonly record stock movements and
          distinguish available from reserved quantities.
          Source: SRC-001.
          Exact context: "Reserved stock is not available for allocation."
          Interpretation: The distinction between available and reserved
          quantity is common in this class of system.
          Epistemic: EXPLICIT. Confidence: HIGH.
          Affected concepts: StockItem, Reservation.

EVD-002   Claim: Reservation may be taken before or after payment depending on
          the organisation.
          Source: SRC-001, SRC-002.
          Exact context: the two sources do not agree on ordering.
          Interpretation: The lifecycle position of reservation relative to
          payment is CONTESTED and cannot be resolved from public sources.
          Epistemic: CONFLICTING. Confidence: MEDIUM.
          Affected concepts: Reservation, Order, Payment.

OpenDomain: Domain-First Engineering                   C   Appendix C – WORKED MICRO-EXAMPLE



XCONF-001   Topic: reservation position relative to payment.
            Source A: SRC-001 (reservation precedes payment).
            Source B: SRC-002 (payment precedes reservation).
            Difference: ordering of two events in the order lifecycle.

C.3 Gate 4 (requirements) – the evidence gate in action
Failed promotion (recorded to demonstrate the gate):
  Source: SRC-003. Finding: a competitor supports reservation.
  -> REQ: "The system SHALL support stock reservation."
  REJECTED: no Candidate Need record. The competitor's capability is
  evidence about the market, not about this organisation's problem.

Successful derivation:
  EVD-001 -> Candidate Need: users need to know what is actually allocatable,
  as distinct from what is physically present.
  -> REQ-014: The system SHALL distinguish, for each item, the quantity
     physically on hand from the quantity allocatable, and SHALL expose both.
  Epistemic: EXPLICIT. Derived from: EVD-001, RULE-004.

Blocked requirement:
  -> REQ-015 (candidate): The system SHALL hold a reservation from order
     confirmation until shipment.
  Status: BLOCKED by XCONF-001. Epistemic: UNKNOWN.
  Resolution required: business decision, or resolution of the conflict.

RULE-004: A reservation may not be created for a quantity exceeding the
allocatable quantity.
Epistemic: INFERRED from EVD-001. Consequence: Gate 4 passes this rule
because it is explicitly marked INFERRED rather than asserted as EXPLICIT.
A later reconciliation can promote it; nothing has claimed it is confirmed.


C.4 Gate 6 (specification) – acceptance criteria
AC-014-01: Given an item with 100 on hand and 30 already reserved
            And a request to reserve 80
            When the reservation is requested
            Then the reservation is rejected
            And the allocatable quantity remains 70

AC-014-02: Given an item with 100 on hand and 0 reserved
            And a request to reserve 100
            When the reservation is requested
            Then allocatable quantity becomes 0
            And a subsequent request for any positive quantity is rejected

AC-014-03: Given a reservation against an item
            When the reservation is released
            Then allocatable quantity increases by the reserved quantity
            And the movement is recorded in the item's movement history

Spec-014 is a BDD/Gherkin specification. Its three criteria cover the normal
case, the boundary case at zero availability, and the release case, which is
the reversal path. Gate 6 checks that a behaviour with a failure mode has an
error or boundary scenario; AC-014-01 and AC-014-02 discharge that check.

C.5 Gate 8 (implementation) – the laundering check
Suppose an engineer, having shipped the feature, edits REQ-014 from
epistemic = INFERRED to epistemic = EXPLICIT without adding an EVD record
citing a source that states it. Gate 8 rule G8-B2 fires:

gate-8/epistemic-laundering
REQ-014 promoted INFERRED -> EXPLICIT with no intervening evidence

This is the check that the framework exists to provide. The engineer has not
written a false requirement; they have made a supported requirement look
confirmed. Without the check, the distinction is invisible, and the
distinction is the entire point.

D.5 Net effect of the mapping
Of the 168 numbered protocol instructions in the three founding documents, the framework
mechanises 61 as gate conditions. The remainder are classified as:
• not mechanisable (30): instructions requiring human judgement with no decidable predicate,
  e.g. “represent complex processes using diagrams”, “avoid creating unnecessary classes”;
• retained as review prompts (48): “consider”, “evaluate”, “question”, phrasings whose value is
  attention-directing rather than checkable;
• superseded by the epistemic lattice (29): instructions against inventing domain knowledge,
  replaced by a single uniform mechanism.
    The ratio is reported because it is a real limitation. A framework that claims to mechanise a
written process method has usually overclaimed. The claim made here is narrower: that 61 of
the tractable prohibitions are now checks, that the remainder are honestly classified, and that no
instruction was silently promoted to a gate it does not support.
    Two exclusions keep the count honest. The 61 counts gate conditions only, not the nine
gates themselves and not the review prompts retained in the gate tables. It also excludes the
six conditions introduced by Sections 9 and 10 (G6-B6, G6-W3, G8-B5, G8-B6, G8-B7, G8-W4),
because none of them derives from the three founding documents; they are the framework’s own
additions and are mapped in D.4. Adding them to the 61 would improve the ratio and misstate
where the conditions came from.
